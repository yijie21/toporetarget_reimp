Laplacian Optimization

The stage that actually transfers the interaction from human to robot — every equation taken apart, with playgrounds you can poke.

★ The whole stage in one sentence

Find the robot joint configuration $q$ whose hand-object mesh has the same local relative geometry as the human's mesh. Everything below is just the precise machinery for defining "local relative geometry."
Analogy to hold onto: imagine describing where someone stands in a group photo. You could give GPS coordinates (absolute) — useless if the photo was taken somewhere else. Or you could say "half a step behind Mia, just left of the lamp" (relative) — that description still works if you recreate the scene in a different room, at a different scale, with different-height people. TopoRetarget copies the relative description, not the GPS.

0 Notation decoder — read this first

Every symbol is built from a base letter + optional superscript (which world it belongs to) + optional subscript (which item / which frame). Decode it like a little address.

Worked example — how to read one symbol:
$v^{\,s}_{\,i,\,t}$
$v$  (base)
a single point's 3-D position (a vertex)
$^{s}$  (superscript)
it lives in the source = human mesh
$_{i,\,t}$  (subscript)
vertex #$i$, at frame #$t$
So $v^s_{i,t}$ = "the position of the $i$-th vertex of the human mesh, in frame $t$." That's the entire trick — once you can read the address, every equation is just these pieces recombined.

Superscripts — "which world?"

SymbolMeans
$\,^{s}$source = the human demonstration (the fixed target)
$\,^{h}$human hand keypoints specifically (e.g. $P^h$)
$\,^{r}$robot (e.g. $V^r$, $q^r$, $P^r$) — the thing we solve for
$\,^{o}$object (e.g. $q^o$ = object pose)
$\,^{base},\,^{\theta}$robot wrist pose vs. robot joint angles
$\,^{*}$the optimal / solved value (e.g. $q^{r,*}_t$)

Subscripts — "which item?"

SymbolMeans
$_{t}$frame number (time step), $t=1\ldots N$
$_{i}$, $_{j}$vertex index (which of the 71 points)
$_{ij}$the edge connecting vertex $i$ to vertex $j$
$_{k}$, $_{l}$bone / keypoint index (used in $E_{\text{bone}}$)
$_{1:N}$the whole trajectory, frame 1 through $N$

1 First: what's fixed, and what are we solving for?

This is the single most important thing to get straight before any equation.

Human mesh
71 vertices at known, fixed positions. It's the recorded demo — nothing to solve. It is the target.
Object samples (×50)
Identical & fixed in both meshes (same object, same pose). They do not depend on $q$. Think of them as push-pins nailed to the table — we'll call them anchors.
Robot hand (×21)
The only unknown. Joint angles $q$ → forward kinematics → keypoint positions $P^r(q)$. These are what the optimizer moves.
Why this matters: because the object anchors are shared and frozen, "match the relative geometry" stops being vague — it forces the robot's fingers into a definite place relative to the object. Hold this thought; it's the secret to the whole transfer (Section 6).

2 Equation ① — Edge weights how much each relationship matters

Not all connections are equally important. A finger touching the object matters far more than a fingertip 10 cm away. Weights encode that.

raw weight, then normalized
$$\tilde{w}_{ij,t}=\exp\!\big(-\kappa\,\lVert v^s_{i,t}-v^s_{j,t}\rVert_2\big)\qquad\qquad w_{ij,t}=\frac{\tilde{w}_{ij,t}}{\sum_{j'\in\mathcal{N}_t(i)}\tilde{w}_{ij',t}}$$
In plain English: for each edge, measure how far apart its endpoints are in the human pose ($v^s$ = source). Convert distance → weight with an exponential drop-off: close = big weight, far = tiny weight. Then rescale each vertex's weights so they sum to 1 (so they behave like a weighted average).

$\kappa$ = decay rate. Big $\kappa$ → "only the nearest neighbour counts." Small $\kappa$ → "everyone counts roughly equally." $v^s$ = human positions → weights are constants (don't depend on $q$). $\mathcal{N}_t(i)$ = the neighbours of vertex $i$ (from the copied edge set $\mathcal{I}_t$).
decay $\kappa$: 0.14
Playground: the center vertex is linked to 5 neighbours. Edge thickness & the % label = its weight. Drag any point, or slide $\kappa$: watch how raising $\kappa$ makes only the closest neighbours keep meaningful weight (the far ones fade to ~0%).
Why use human distances and freeze the weights? Two reasons: (1) the human demo is the ground-truth interaction we want to preserve, so its proximities define what's important; (2) keeping the weights constant (not functions of $q$) makes the optimization a clean least-squares problem. The big payoff: contacts automatically dominate the objective, and incidental far links are ignored — no manual contact labelling needed.

3 Equation ② — The Laplacian coordinate the "local shape" of a point

This single arrow is the heart of the method.

Laplacian / differential coordinate of vertex $i$
$$\Delta_t(V)_i=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_t(i)}w_{ij,t}\,(v_i-v_j)\;=\;\underbrace{v_i}_{\text{the point}}\;-\;\underbrace{\sum_j w_{ij,t}\,v_j}_{\text{weighted avg of its neighbours}}$$
In plain English: $\Delta_i$ is the arrow pointing from the weighted center of a vertex's neighbours to the vertex itself. It answers one question: "how far, and in which direction, does this point stick out from its local crowd?"

Property 1 — translation-free
Slide the whole mesh anywhere and $\Delta_i$ doesn't change (the shift cancels in $v_i-v_j$). So it captures relative structure, not absolute position — exactly what survives a different-sized hand.
Property 2 — encodes contact
For a fingertip whose neighbours include nearby object samples, $\Delta$ is the tip's offset from the surface — how far off the object it sits, and in which direction. Preserve that arrow → preserve the contact.
The bold pink arrow is $\Delta$ of the orange vertex (from the grey weighted-center to the vertex). Drag points to reshape it. Press "Translate" to slide everything — notice $\Delta$ stays identical, proving it only describes relative placement.
Analogy: $\Delta$ is your "I'm standing just here relative to my neighbours" description. Two completely different rooms can host the same arrangement — same $\Delta$ for everyone — even if the absolute coordinates are nowhere near each other.

4 Equation ③ — The objective $E_{\text{IM}}$ match every local shape

interaction-mesh energy (the thing we minimize over $q$)
$$E_{\text{IM}}(q)=\frac{1}{N_v}\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}\big\lVert\,\underbrace{\Delta_t\big(V^r_t(q)\big)_i}_{\text{robot's arrow (depends on }q)}\;-\;\underbrace{\Delta_t\big(V^s_t\big)_i}_{\text{human's arrow (fixed target)}}\big\rVert_2^2$$
In plain English: for all 71 vertices, compute the robot's local-shape arrow and compare it to the human's. Square the gap, average. Driving $E_{\text{IM}}\!\to\!0$ means every robot vertex sits in the same relative spot to its neighbours as the human's did — same hand shape and same contact, on a different hand.

The same weights appear on both sides. The constants $w_{ij}$ from Eq.① are baked into both $\Delta(V^r)$ and $\Delta(V^s)$. So you're applying one fixed linear operator (the weighted graph Laplacian) to two different vertex layouts and asking the outputs to agree. That's what makes it a fair, apples-to-apples comparison.

5 How these three transfer the interaction the payoff

Here's the subtle bit that ties it together — and why the object anchors are everything.

A Laplacian coordinate is translation-free (Property 1). On its own that's a problem: matching arrows would only recover the hand's shape up to a free floating position — the hand could drift anywhere. What nails it down is the shared, fixed object samples:

The anchor trick: the 50 object points are identical in both meshes and are not controlled by $q$. They're push-pins. When you minimize $E_{\text{IM}}$, those anchors hold the coordinate frame still, so matching the $\Delta$'s forces the robot's fingers into the correct absolute position relative to the object. No drift. The interaction is pinned down by the object itself.
Robot strategy:
Left = human (fixed target, fingertips resting cleanly on the object). Right = robot, a bigger hand. Green dots = object anchors (same in both). Try the three strategies:
• Bone-copy (Stage 1 only): right finger angles, but longer fingers overshoot → penetration.
• Absolute tip-match (DexPilot-style): forces tips to the human's raw coordinates → tips float / drift off this object.
• Laplacian: matches each tip's relative offset to the anchors → lands cleanly on the surface.
What "Animate optimization" shows: the optimizer starts from the bone-copy guess (penetrating) and drives $E_{\text{IM}}$ downward — the robot fingertips slide out of the object onto the surface, reaching the same relative contact the human had. That descent is the interaction transfer.

6 Reading it by edge type

The objective touches three kinds of edges, and each one does a different job.

Edge typeWeightWhat matching its $\Delta$ achieves
object–objectn/aIdentical in both meshes → error ≈ 0. These are the anchors that pin the frame.
hand–handmediumPreserves the robot's intra-hand shape — the finger pose / articulation stays human-like.
hand–object (cross)high (close!)Preserves where each finger sits relative to the object surface → this is the contact / interaction transfer.

Because cross-edges at contacts are short, Eq.① hands them big weights — so the optimization naturally prioritizes getting the grasp right over distant, irrelevant relationships.

7 How it's actually solved (and where it plugs in)

Nonlinear least squares in $q$. Forward kinematics makes $\Delta(V^r(q))$ nonlinear in the joints, so $E_{\text{IM}}$ is minimized with an iterative least-squares solver.
Warm-started by Stage 1. The bone-direction guess gets the hand near a human-like pose first, so the solve doesn't fall into a weird local minimum (and the copied edges stay meaningful).
Per frame, ~4.7 ms. Fast enough to be real-time-capable; each frame is also regularized toward the previous one for smooth motion.

In the real system $E_{\text{IM}}$ isn't minimized alone — it's one term in the full per-frame problem, alongside human-like finger bends, smoothness, and a hard no-penetration constraint:

$$\big(q^{r,*}_t,s^*_t\big)=\arg\min_{q,s}\;\lambda_{\text{IM}}E_{\text{IM}}(q)+\lambda_{\text{bone}}E_{\text{bone}}(q)+E_{\text{reg}}(q;q^{r,*}_{t-1})+\tfrac{w_s}{2}\!\!\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}_t}\!s_i^2$$
$$\text{s.t.}\quad \varphi_i(q)\ge -b,\qquad \varphi_i(q)+s_i\ge -\tau,\qquad 0\le s_i\le b-\tau$$
Read it as: reproduce the human's local interaction ($E_{\text{IM}}$), with human-like finger articulation ($E_{\text{bone}}$), smoothly over time ($E_{\text{reg}}$), while physically never penetrating the object (the $\varphi$ constraints with slack $s_i$). $E_{\text{IM}}$ is the part that carries "same interaction"; the rest keep it natural and feasible.

The one-line takeaway: by matching weighted local arrows ($\Delta$) against a frame pinned by object anchors, the robot is placed at the same relative contact as the human — automatically adapting to a bigger or differently-shaped hand instead of being forced to an impossible absolute fingertip location.
